Photographing a small space means making information clear from a position that is often severely limited. The viewer needs to identify the fittings and understand part of their arrangement, even when the photograph does not show the entire room. The usefulness of the framing comes from what it communicates.

Showing the essential elements does not always require including every wall, the whole floor and the ceiling. In my [residential and hospitality photography](/en/residential-hospitality/), I aim to retain enough reference points for the layout to remain understandable. A narrow bathroom provides a practical example of working with the space actually available.

## Establish what the photograph needs to communicate

I took this photograph in a hotel where the bathrooms were very narrow. I wanted to show the fittings: the shower, basin and mirror. Those were the priority pieces of information for this view.

In a small space, my aim is to show all the useful elements and part of the configuration. I do the best I can with what the location allows. That approach begins with a practical question: from the accessible position, what can remain within the frame to explain the arrangement?

Here, the shower occupies the left-hand side, with the basin on the right beneath the large mirror. Including them together makes the relationship between the fittings readable. The photograph therefore provides more information than a sequence of isolated details, while remaining a partial view of the room.

That starting point guides how I assess the framing. The amount of wall or floor visible matters less than being able to recognise the fittings and locate them in relation to one another.

## Find more distance when the room sets the limit

The lack of space did constrain the photography. If I remember correctly, I had taken the door off its hinges to gain more room to step back. In this kind of situation, access to the room is one of the things to consider before framing the image.

When fittings are close to the camera, the available position determines a substantial part of the result. A little extra distance can change how much of the shower is visible, the basin’s position within the photograph, or how their outlines fit into the frame.

The work therefore begins with the actual possibilities on site. I need to consider which positions allow the different elements to be seen together. In this bathroom, the image boundaries were constrained by the space; they did not result from an entirely free choice among many viewpoints.

That is an important aspect of photographing a small room. Framing sometimes develops through modest adjustments, with very little freedom to move. A useful photograph can emerge from that practical adaptation to the dimensions of the space.

## Use a wide view from the available position

In these situations, I use the shortest focal length available to me, which is 15 mm in my case. The field of view allows more elements to be included from a position where stepping back is difficult. It is a practical tool for responding to the limitations of the room.

The field of view needs to be distinguished from perspective. Perspective relationships depend on the viewpoint and the camera’s distance from the different elements. The focal length then determines what fits into the image from that position.

This distinction helps clarify the available options. Changing focal length from a given position changes the framing; being able to step back also changes the camera’s relationship to the fittings. Those two considerations work together within the limits imposed by the room.

I discuss these questions in my article on [focal lengths and the perception of architectural volumes](/en/post/focal-length-architecture-photography-volume-perception/). For this photograph, the essential point is using a wide field of view to show the shower, basin and mirror with the distance available.

## Connect the fittings through layout reference points

A shower and basin can be recognisable without their relationship being clear. To provide an impression of the configuration, the frame also needs to retain architectural elements that help locate them.

In this photograph, the glazed screen defines the shower area. The tiled section beside it provides a transition towards the basin. The large mirror sits above the basin against a background with a wood-like appearance. Those reference points organise the view from left to right.

The shower is identified through its fittings and screen. The basin remains readable through its shape and relationship to the tap. The mirror supplies another reference point higher in the frame. Each element therefore has enough presence for the viewer to understand what is provided in this space.

The frame does not need to describe every fitting from every angle. It needs to retain enough information for its function and position to be understood. That reading responds to my initial intention: showing the fittings together with part of their arrangement.

## Keep a fairly frontal view around the mirror

I chose to frame the photograph fairly frontally in relation to the mirror. Its reflection did not have a particular role in that choice. The mirror was primarily one of the fittings I wanted to show in this bathroom.

Its position above the basin creates an easily identifiable grouping. The eye can move from basin to tap, then to mirror, before returning towards the shower. The photograph brings those elements together while keeping their relationships understandable.

I had not sought a particular effect through the reflection. My main aim was to include this fitting in the view of the room. Its rounded outline distinguishes it from the tile pattern and the lines of the glazed screen, while keeping it connected to the basin beneath.

Broader questions about reflections and restricted space are covered in my article on [bathroom photography](/en/post/bathroom-photography-mastering-reflections-tight-spaces/). Here, the mirror mainly contributes to reading the fittings together within one view.

## Assess the result through what the viewer can understand

For this kind of small space, I judge the photograph first by the information it retains. Is the shower identifiable? Are the basin and mirror visible? Can their arrangement be understood from the reference points included in the frame?

Looking at the image this way brings the work back to its purpose. A partial view can be useful when it brings the fittings together and provides a clear impression of their configuration. The frame remains that of a photograph made in a room where space is limited.

My experience of this bathroom centres on that adaptation: finding the available distance, using a sufficiently wide view and retaining the elements that explain the layout. The result develops from what the location permits, in service of the information that needs to be shown.
