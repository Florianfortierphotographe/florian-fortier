An illuminated passageway can reveal the organisation of an entire architectural composition. Lighting outlines an opening, separates columns from their surroundings and draws attention to a feature further away. Photographing that lighting means making these relationships clear: which elements connect, how the different planes fit together and what role the darker areas retain within the image.

For an architect, lighting designer or project owner, a photograph can therefore explain how lighting contributes to the perception of a development. In my [architectural photography](/en/architecture/), I use viewpoint and framing to convey this organisation. Showing that the lights are on is only a starting point. The image needs to explain what their presence brings to the architecture and how the illuminated elements work together.

## Begin with what the client wants to communicate

The photograph of this covered passageway responded to a client request to showcase the development’s scenography. Lighting was one of its defining features and was already in place. My photographic task was to show that existing arrangement and the relationships it established between different architectural elements.

I specifically wanted to highlight how the lighting brought out the arch and the fountain. These features occupy different planes: the arch belongs to the passageway, while the fountain stands further away in the courtyard. Including both in one image explains their relationship, giving the photograph a subject that extends beyond either individual feature.

This intention provides a practical guide to framing. If the arch dominates the entire photograph, the fountain can become too insignificant to register. A close view of the fountain alone would lose the passageway through which it is seen. Each needs enough presence for the viewer to understand the connection, with the surrounding architecture supporting that reading.

## Connect the arch and fountain within a single view

In this photograph, the illuminated outline of the arch defines the upper part of the opening. Further away, the fountain provides a focal point in the centre of the courtyard. The eye can move between them through the spaces between the columns. The passageway therefore helps locate the fountain within its architectural setting.

The strength of the composition lies in the continuity between these two subjects. The arch frames the view while keeping it open to the courtyard. The fountain gives the eye somewhere to settle without overwhelming the elements in front of it. Lighting makes both features visible at different distances, and the framing brings those points of emphasis into one coherent image.

The surroundings also have a useful role. The façades beyond the opening establish that the fountain belongs to a courtyard. They remain quieter than the illuminated passageway. Giving every element equal visual weight would pull attention away from the relationship I want to show and make the overall lighting arrangement harder to read.

## Choose the camera position before refining the frame

I selected the viewpoint to emphasise depth, using a relatively long focal length with the intention of compressing the planes. That description needs a qualification: perspective depends on viewpoint, meaning the camera’s position relative to the different elements. The focal length then determines the framing available from that position.

Changing focal length while keeping the camera in the same place changes the field of view; it does not, by itself, change the perspective relationships. The apparent closeness of the planes is associated with the shooting distance. Here, viewpoint and framing work together to give the fountain a clear presence while retaining the arch and columns around it.

Depth remains legible through the sequence of elements: the nearer columns, those further along the passageway, then the fountain and façades. A compressed appearance can therefore coexist with a clear understanding of which elements sit in front of others. I discuss the broader framing choices in my article on [focal lengths and the perception of architectural volumes](/en/post/focal-length-architecture-photography-volume-perception/).

## Let the columns support the lighting composition

The illuminated columns occupy a substantial part of both sides of the photograph. They make the covered passageway immediately recognisable and accompany the opening towards the courtyard. Their repetition also helps separate the different planes, even though the bright surfaces attract considerable attention in their own right.

The nearer columns have a strong visual presence in this image, but the fountain still needs to remain visible between them. Looking at the gaps is therefore as useful as looking at the solid forms. The space between two columns determines what can be seen beyond them. This is where the lighting arrangement and the photographic composition meet in a very concrete way.

The ground contributes to the same continuity. Its lines lead towards the courtyard and give the composition a base. Together, these visual cues allow the lighting to be understood as part of a three-dimensional space, with volumes and distances that remain readable throughout the frame.

## Use different exposures to retain visual hierarchy

I made several exposures and then recovered the information I needed in Photoshop. Working with these exposures gave me different information about the brighter and darker parts of the scene. The decisions about what to retain in the final photograph remain guided by the image and by the scenography it needs to communicate.

The passageway and courtyard have different brightness levels. The final rendering needs to preserve the illuminated forms while allowing quieter areas to retain their supporting role. Keeping that hierarchy helps explain the effect of the lighting: which features it emphasises, which remain in the background and how attention moves through the space.

I do not aim to make every area equally bright. A darker courtyard can still contain enough information to locate the fountain and surrounding façades. The brighter areas also need information so that the arch and columns keep their visible relief. My article on [exposure bracketing in architectural photography](/en/post/exposure-bracketing-architecture-photography/) explains the wider value of photographing a scene at different exposures. Here, that approach serves the specific relationship between the illuminated passageway and the courtyard beyond it.

## Combine daytime and night-time views in the series

I also photographed a daytime version. The night-time image adds a view of the development’s lighting and the features it highlights. The two photographs offer complementary readings of the same place: its appearance in daylight and the way its architectural lighting presents it at night.

This combination is useful when a lighting arrangement forms part of a development’s identity. A daytime view alone cannot fully communicate its effect. The night-time photograph adds that information to the series, allowing another aspect of the completed project to be shown and understood alongside its daytime appearance.

In this passageway, the connection between the arch and the fountain gives the image its subject. Viewpoint, framing and exposure treatment all help make that connection clear.
