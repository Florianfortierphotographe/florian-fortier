A staircase looks different depending on where it is viewed from. Looking down from a landing can bring the steps, changes of direction and lower flights into a single frame. It can make an arrangement easier to understand than a view taken from just one side.

For a developer wanting to highlight this architectural feature, the benefit is practical: its form and the presence of its material can be shown in the same photograph. In my [architectural photography](/en/architecture/), I choose this kind of framing when looking down provides useful reference points for understanding how the subject is organised.

## Respond to a specific request from the developer

I made this image during a photographic assignment covering a property development. The developer wanted to highlight the staircase. It was therefore a photograph devoted to one particular architectural feature within the wider commission.

My intention was to show its configuration and material. Those two aspects come together in the framing: the steps and handrails describe the staircase’s shape, while the timber surfaces make its material clearly visible. The photograph needs to allow those pieces of information to be read together.

Here, describing the staircase as a detail refers to its place within the overall assignment. The frame remains wide enough to show several parts of the structure and their relationships. The overall form matters as much as the surfaces that can be seen. The connection between the chosen feature and its arrangement gives the image its direction.

## Look down from the landing to reveal changes of direction

I took this photograph from the upstairs landing, directing the camera downwards. I chose that view to highlight the staircase’s configuration, its shape and the material from which it was made. The landing provided a viewpoint over the sequence of visible parts.

In the photograph, the nearest steps occupy the right-hand edge. The eye then meets the turn further away and discovers more steps below. The change of direction becomes apparent through that sequence. The downward view brings elements at different heights together within one image, allowing their positions to be compared.

This reading depends on the camera’s position and orientation. A view from above can still be difficult to understand if a handrail obscures everything underneath it. The value of looking down therefore comes from the relationships revealed between parts of the staircase, beyond the impression of height alone.

## Keep reference points around the central opening

The central opening allows the eye to travel down into the photograph. The steps and handrails surrounding it provide the reference points needed to understand that depth. Their apparent sizes and overlaps distinguish nearby elements from those further below, helping the viewer follow the arrangement.

A clearly visible tread establishes a direction. A change in orientation indicates a turn. A section of handrail continues the visual route towards another part of the staircase. Together, these clues give continuity to the way the eye moves through the image.

I aim to retain enough of those reference points for the photograph to remain understandable. If the opening occupied almost the entire frame, the geometry around it would become secondary. If nearby elements covered it completely, the lower flights would be difficult to locate. The framing needs to show how the different parts are arranged around that space.

## Show the timber within the overall structure

Timber occupies a substantial part of this photograph. Its grain is visible across the treads, horizontal surfaces and handrails. The white walls help distinguish their outlines, while the balusters introduce finer lines between the wooden elements.

That material presence directly reflects my intention. The viewer can examine the surfaces while following the staircase’s form. The timber can therefore be read through elements that each have a place in its configuration: a tread, a turn or a handrail.

The view provides different information from a close-up limited to wood grain. It shows where the timber appears and how it accompanies the changes of direction. This specific question complements my broader approach to [staircase photography](/en/post/staircase-architecture-photography-sculpture-light-use/), with the emphasis here on looking down from the landing.

## Observe what the railings allow you to see

I do not think the railings caused any particular difficulty for this image. They can, however, be a real challenge when I photograph staircases from above. A handrail close to the camera quickly occupies a significant part of the frame and can obscure the descent beneath it.

In this view, the railings frame an opening towards the flights below. Their presence helps establish distances, but the gaps between them matter too. It is through those spaces that the next parts of the staircase become visible.

When preparing this kind of framing, I therefore look at the openings as closely as the solid elements. The directions need to remain traceable without all the lines merging. A clear composition depends on the position of the visible steps and on the spaces left for the eye to move between the rails.

## Seek a centred viewpoint with enough height

My advice is to try positioning yourself centrally in relation to the subject and to gain height where doing so improves the view. A higher camera position can reveal tread surfaces and make changes of direction easier to distinguish. Centring helps distribute the elements around the opening.

The next step is to assess what that position actually contributes to the frame. Being centred does not mean achieving perfect symmetry: flights, landings and handrails do not necessarily present themselves identically on either side. The purpose is to make their arrangement clear to the viewer.

The camera can be tilted towards the opening from a stable position on the landing, with the tripod properly supported and the equipment secured. Seeking a higher viewpoint needs to remain compatible with a protected position behind the railing. These choices extend the principles of [architectural composition](/en/post/secrets-good-composition-architecture-photography/) to the particular requirements of looking down through a stairwell.

## Select the view that adds information to the assignment

For the assignment I had been given, no other viewpoint on this staircase added anything of real value to the photographic coverage. That assessment relates to the brief: the aim was to highlight this feature, its configuration and its material.

The view from the landing brought those pieces of information together. It showed the connected parts of the staircase and gave the timber a substantial presence. Its value lay in what it made understandable about the feature the developer wanted to present.
