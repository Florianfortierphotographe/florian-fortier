# Photographing Changes in Ceiling Height: Making a Living Space Easy to Understand

Ceiling height can vary considerably within a living space. A sitting area open to the floor above may sit alongside a dining room or kitchen beneath an upper floor. This arrangement gives a home character, yet a photograph can easily flatten those differences. Framing only the furniture and floor explains how the room is used while revealing little about its vertical organisation.

To show changes in height, I look for references that explain the space: the edge of the upper floor, its underside, the opening above and the features within the living room. Their relationship matters more than a general impression of spaciousness. In photography for a property sale, a clear view of this arrangement helps prospective buyers understand a layout they will later discover in person.

## Showing what makes this home distinctive

The photograph illustrating this article was taken for the sale of a property. The building is a former chapel converted into a home. I wanted to show the room’s volume, its height and the presence of an opening onto the first floor. These characteristics are an important part of what makes the property distinctive.

I describe it as an opening onto the first floor rather than a mezzanine. That distinction matters: a photograph reveals part of the building’s organisation, but cannot describe the entire upper level. The language used to present the home needs to remain faithful to its layout rather than suggesting a different arrangement.

The chapel’s former use provides context. For this view, my attention is on what is visible and useful for presenting the property: the living room, fireplace, furniture and relationship with the floor above. The purpose is to make their coexistence within one residential space understandable. The image needs to connect these features so viewers can see how the room works as a whole.

## Choosing a corner viewpoint to connect the room’s features

I positioned the camera in a corner to show the fireplace, the room’s volume, the opening above and the furniture. That position brings several pieces of information into one overall view. The fireplace appears on the right, seating occupies the foreground, and the dining area and kitchen can be seen further back.

This viewpoint establishes a relationship between nearby features and the spaces beyond. The eye can move from the sitting area towards the part beneath the upper floor while retaining a reference to the opening above. The framing shows how the furniture occupies the room rather than simply presenting a sequence of objects.

In my [property photography](/en/residential-hospitality/), I choose the camera position according to the features that need to be presented. Here, moving into the corner served a specific intention: connecting the living room’s height with its furnishings, while giving the opening above a clear place within that broader view of the interior.

## Keeping the transition between heights visible

The underside of the upper floor creates a clear boundary in this photograph. It covers the rear part of the room and marks the transition to a lower space. Above it, the balustrade helps identify the opening onto the first floor. Together, these features explain the vertical arrangement, while the floor and furniture maintain continuity between the different areas below.

That transition is important to understanding the property. A photograph directed only upwards might reveal the upper floor while explaining little about the space underneath. Lower framing could present the living room and kitchen while losing the difference in height. Keeping their meeting point visible allows the arrangement to be understood within a single image.

This is a more specific question than photographing a [double-height space](/en/post/revealing-double-height-volumes-architecture-photography/). Here, the aim is to show where the height changes and how that variation relates to the living area. The built boundaries provide references, giving viewers information they can follow within the picture rather than leaving the layout dependent on what lies outside it.

## Using a 24 mm TS lens to include the height

I did not encounter any particular difficulty framing this image. I used a 24 mm TS lens to show the height. That choice allowed me to work on the overall view while keeping useful information about the opening and the living room arrangement within the frame.

A TS lens offers shift movement, among its capabilities: the framing can move relative to the lens axis, which can help include an upper area with less camera tilt. This capability works alongside the choice of camera position. Perspective and the relationship between near and distant features depend primarily on viewpoint and the distance to those elements.

For this kind of room, I would advise a measured approach to wide-angle lenses: 24 mm, with 20 mm as the widest I would suggest depending on the situation. That is a practical reference from my own work. Trying to include ever more of the room can give the foreground too much prominence. I prefer to select the relationships that matter and check that the proportions remain credible.

## Keeping furniture as a reference within the volume

The furniture matters in this view because I wanted to include it alongside the fireplace and the opening above. It helps viewers recognise a sitting area, a dining area and the kitchen at the back. The volume remains connected to everyday residential use, with familiar features that provide references within the room.

The fireplace also provides a vertical reference beside the higher part of the space. Its presence in the composition connects the living area to the wall rising towards the first floor. Furniture and architecture work together: the seating locates the area for relaxing, while the edge of the upper floor explains the arrangement overhead.

The photograph therefore presents both a layout and a volume. I can show the height while retaining the features that explain the room’s use. This combination is particularly useful for a home whose configuration differs from a rectangular room beneath a ceiling of uniform height. The differences need to remain visible alongside the familiar elements of a living space.
