When photographing a residential development, a useful viewpoint can sometimes be found in another building within the same complex. An upper floor opposite the subject, or slightly to one side, may reveal aspects of the façades that are difficult to see from ground level. The position needs to be identified and accessible when the shoot takes place.

The photograph shown here was taken from another building within the development. The elevated position reveals the balconies, the setbacks of the upper floors and the roofs. Vegetation occupies part of the foreground, while neighbouring buildings place the project within its surroundings.

In an [architectural photography assignment](/en/architecture/), this kind of view deserves to be planned with the client. Its value depends both on what it communicates and on the practical arrangements needed to produce it.

## Define What the Viewpoint Should Contribute

Before looking for access, I establish what the photograph needs to explain. For a developer or architect, the priority might be to show how the building's volumes fit together, the sequence of balconies or the position of terraces on the upper floors.

This purpose provides a basis for comparing possible locations. If the uppermost setback is the main subject, a low position may leave it hidden behind the façade. If the image needs to explain the entrance and surrounding ground, an elevated view may exclude those features instead.

In this photograph, the upper parts of the building are clearly visible, while trees conceal some of the lower levels. That limitation matters when selecting images for the final series. Defining the photograph's role from the beginning helps identify a useful position and plan the complementary views needed to explain the rest of the development.

## Assess the Building, Floor and Lateral Position

The closest building does not necessarily offer the most useful angle. Its distance, height and position relative to the subject determine which parts of the project will be visible. I consider these three factors together.

Two apartments on the same floor can offer quite different views. One position may reveal a side elevation; another may cause several balconies to overlap, making their depth harder to read. Moving higher may expose a roof while reducing the visual presence of the façades.

Site plans and information from the teams working on the development can help identify positions worth checking. A reference photograph taken from the intended opening then allows the proposed view to be assessed against the actual scene. This develops the approach discussed in my article on [scouting before an architecture shoot](/en/post/importance-scouting-before-architecture-real-estate-shoot/): the specific task here is to find a usable camera position within a particular building.

## Arrange Specific Access with the Development Team

When several buildings belong to the same development, the client can help establish contact with the people who coordinate access. I seek a contact who can confirm the building, floor and apartment involved.

A general assurance that the building opposite will be accessible still leaves practical details to resolve. Which entrance should I use? Who will be there to open the apartment? Will it remain available throughout the agreed appointment? Clarifying these points avoids spending time finding the right route on the day.

I also explain the request in concrete terms: photographing the development from a defined position, with the necessary equipment and an estimated duration. This helps the team organise the visit around the project's progress. Access becomes part of the schedule prepared with the client, alongside the spaces to be photographed in the main building, with a clear meeting point and contact person.

## Check the Space Available for the Camera

A clear view to the eye does not guarantee that the camera can occupy the required position. On a balcony or terrace, a railing, side partition or projecting wall can enter the composition. There must also be enough room for the photographer and tripod.

I check where it is possible to set up, how far back I can stand and how much movement is available. A small shift may change the overlap between a branch and a balcony, or introduce the edge of a wall into the foreground.

If the photograph is taken from a window, its opening mechanism and any glass remaining between the camera and subject also matter. Reflections or marks on the glass can affect the result. This assessment allows equipment to be chosen for the actual location and avoids planning the shoot around a position that cannot be used as intended.

## Coordinate the Shoot with Apartment Handover

Access can change quickly in a development where apartments are being handed over. A space available during scouting may be needed for finishing work, a visit or its new occupants by the time photography takes place.

It is therefore useful to discuss this viewpoint early in the planning process. With the team, I look for a time when access is possible, the building being photographed is ready and the light suits the façade. The chosen appointment needs to account for all three factors.

Before the shoot, I confirm the access arrangements and the person to contact on arrival. If an apartment handover changes availability, another position can be considered. Planning an alternative from spaces that are already accessible gives the schedule some flexibility. The wider assignment can then continue if the elevated photograph needs to be postponed or replaced with another view of the building.

## Build the Composition Within the Available Position

On arrival, I compare the planned composition with the scene in front of me. Camera height, vegetation and background buildings affect how clearly the architectural volumes separate. I check particularly that the balconies and setbacks remain easy to understand.

Focal length then allows me to adjust how much of the scene appears in the photograph. It cannot replace a change of position when two elements overlap: their alignment depends on where the camera stands. I test the movements available within the space and select the views that explain the architecture most clearly.

I also consider vertical lines and the amount of sky or vegetation retained. In a [dense urban environment](/en/post/photographing-architecture-dense-urban-environments/), neighbouring buildings can provide useful context. The composition should make the subject identifiable and its volumes readable, with a considered balance between the development, its foreground and the buildings behind it.

## Give the Photograph a Clear Role in the Series

A view from another building contributes a particular understanding of the project. It may explain the upper floors or connect several volumes within one image. That purpose should remain clear when assembling the final series for the client.

I combine it with photographs taken from entrances, pathways and shared spaces. These views restore a pedestrian sense of scale and explain features that disappear from the elevated composition. Together, they give the client a more useful selection for presenting the development as a whole.
