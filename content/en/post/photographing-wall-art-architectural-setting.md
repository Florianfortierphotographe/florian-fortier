A large framed image can give an entrance hall a distinctive presence. Its colours draw attention, its format relates to the size of the wall, and its position shapes the way people discover the space. Photographing it calls for attention to both the image itself and the way it has been installed.

In this photograph, the composition in blue, brown and white occupies the left wall. The glazed door, pale veined floor and wood-toned wall finish establish its surroundings. Together, they give the decorative feature a sense of scale and place the viewer within the hall.

In my [architecture and interior design photography](/en/architecture/), I aim to preserve this relationship. The photograph should make the image readable and help the viewer understand its contribution to the interior.

## Establish What the Decoration Should Communicate

Before choosing a composition, I consider the client's purpose in presenting the decorative feature. Is the priority to show its placement, document an interior designer's scheme or illustrate a manufacturer's work? The photographs required will depend on that emphasis.

An interior designer may want to explain how the size of the image relates to the room's lines. A decorative specialist might need a photograph that allows closer examination of its graphic content. Both requirements can belong within the same brief.

On site, I look at observable relationships: the distance from an opening, the hanging height, an alignment with another feature or the contrast with an adjacent wall. I discuss these with the client, without assigning intentions to the designer that they have not expressed. This extends my approach to [details that reveal a designer's style](/en/post/decorative-details-highlighting-designer-style/), with a specific question: how can the photograph explain the decoration at the scale of the room?

## Retain References That Make the Size Understandable

A close crop provides little information about the actual dimensions of a decorative image. Including the floor, a door or part of the ceiling helps the viewer judge its presence in the space. They can also understand whether it accompanies a circulation route or occupies an entire wall.

In the photograph shown here, the dark frame remains visible on all four sides. The nearby door provides a familiar reference, while the floor establishes the position of the frame's lower edge. These elements make the installation easier to understand.

I choose how much space to include around the subject according to the information it provides. A narrow area of wall may explain how the image is mounted; a wider view becomes useful when several features relate to it. The aim is to retain enough context to communicate its size while giving the decorative image a clear presence within the composition.

## Choose a Viewpoint That Suits the Proportions

Camera position changes the appearance of a rectangular decorative feature. An oblique view shows how it sits within the room, but its sides may appear to converge. A frontal view makes its geometry and content easier to read.

When photographing an interior scheme, I may retain an angled viewpoint if it helps explain the depth of the hall. I then check that the image remains legible and that the frame does not look excessively distorted. Available working distance and the position of openings inform that decision.

If the client needs to document the decorative feature's shape, an additional photograph taken directly opposite its surface will be more suitable. Correcting vertical lines in a wider photograph does not automatically turn it into a frontal reproduction. Planning both camera positions during the shoot avoids relying on a single composition to meet requirements that call for different views.

## Preserve the Colours Within the Room's Atmosphere

Wall art has its own palette, which sits alongside the colours of the interior. Here, the blues in the image appear beside wood tones and pale surfaces. This relationship deserves to be preserved during editing.

I take care not to increase the decoration's saturation so much that its balance with the hall changes. A strong colour can attract attention without additional emphasis. Quieter shades also contribute to the way the composition is read and should retain their place within it.

Ambient lighting requires particular attention, as several sources can introduce different colour casts across a wall. Global colour adjustments must therefore be checked within the decorative image as well. An adjustment intended to warm the room could shift its blues or whites. The [balance between realism and emotion in interior photography](/en/post/color-interior-photography-balance-realism-emotion/) depends here on measured adjustments that take both the decoration and the surrounding space into account.

## Examine Reflections Before Changing the Lighting

Decorative surfaces respond differently to light according to their finish. A matt surface, a glossy panel and an image behind glass each require a different assessment. I examine that response on site before settling on the camera position.

If a reflection obscures part of the design, moving the camera slightly may improve its readability. At the same time, I check the frame's proportions and the effect of that movement on the rest of the room. The solution has to work across the whole photograph.

The room's lighting also belongs to the design being presented. Switching off a wall light might reduce a distracting reflection while removing useful information about the atmosphere. Where several lighting arrangements are needed, I plan them with the client. I also distinguish actual reflections from highlights within the graphic composition: a pale area that belongs to the image should remain part of it.

## Pair the Contextual View with a Complementary Photograph

A short sequence can present the decoration at several scales. The first photograph explains its location and its relationship with the architecture. A second, more frontal image offers a clearer reading of the graphic composition. A detail is useful when the frame, its junction with the wall or a particular finish deserves attention.

Each photograph should provide identifiable information. Two similar compositions taken a few steps apart do not necessarily answer separate needs. I prefer to establish what each view will communicate before increasing the number of photographs.

This approach also helps when putting together a portfolio or project page. The wider photograph introduces the setting, while the closer image accompanies an explanation of the decorative feature. During selection, I check colour consistency across the sequence so that the viewer recognises the same image from one photograph to the next.

## Discuss the Intended Uses at the Briefing Stage

The intended formats influence preparation for the shoot. A photograph for a website banner needs to keep the decorative feature within an area that will survive the planned crop. A project page may benefit from several formats, combining a general view with a more descriptive image.

If the client needs a precise reproduction for a catalogue, that requirement should be stated in advance. It may call for a dedicated photograph, colour references and specific lighting conditions. An installation photograph gives priority to the relationship between the decoration and its surroundings.
